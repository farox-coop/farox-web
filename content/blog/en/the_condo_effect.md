There's a phenomenon I've seen many times whenever a group of people shares responsibility for something, when something belongs to everyone, yet somehow feels like it belongs to no one.

I like to call it: **The Condo Effect**.

It's quite simple.

Imagine we all live in the same apartment building. There's a hallway everyone uses, and everyone wants it to be clean.

But who sweeps it?

When responsibility is shared, an interesting tension emerges. **What belongs to everyone can easily end up feeling like it belongs to no one.**

If we bring this metaphor into a traditional organization, that tension is usually resolved through hierarchy: someone assigns the task, someone else performs it, and someone else supervises it.

In a cooperative, things work differently.

A horizontal organizational structure has both strengths and weaknesses.  
In some ways it's remarkably flexible; in others, surprisingly rigid.

Responsibilities don't disappear, but the figure responsible for assigning them often does. That raises an inevitable question: **How do roles emerge when nobody explicitly assigns them?**

If nobody is in charge, but there's no clear allocation of responsibilities either, do we simply live in a cloud of tickets where, somehow, a task gently lands in my lap like an autumn leaf and I magically decide to take ownership of it?

## Roles don't appear on an organizational chart

My years at Farox have shown me that roles don't necessarily emerge because someone defines them. **They emerge because someone starts doing certain things repeatedly.**

The moment we become part of a group, we naturally take on some roles while avoiding others. What we do every day gradually shapes the role we occupy.

If you're the one answering emails, you become the person who answers emails.  
If you're the one writing meeting notes, you become the person who writes meeting notes.  
**There is no official appointment.**

We're social creatures. Creatures of habit. Organizations, just like people, develop habits and routines that eventually define responsibilities.

## An example always helps

Some time ago, at our office, we decided to organize cleaning duties. Every two months, a different person would be responsible. After about a year, I did the math. I'd cleaned six times.

What struck me wasn't the number. It was realizing that **I'd become committed to the task, not to the purpose behind it**. I was committed to following the schedule. I'd stopped thinking about why we were cleaning and focused only on checking off my turn.

That shift happens much more often than we realize. Organizations frequently maintain processes that once made perfect sense but, over time, survive simply because "that's how we've always done it."

## Organizations are systems too

Organizations, people, and teams are all complex systems. They don't function simply because someone gives instructions. They function because agreements, responsibilities, and coordination mechanisms emerge, defining roles, often implicitly.

Have you ever seen a ship with ten captains?
Cooperatives could easily become that.  
But they don't.  
They're not captainless ships either.  
**There's an invisible network of responsibilities and areas of ownership.**  
There are people accountable for different things.  
Leadership emerges depending on the problem that needs to be solved.

Some people make technical decisions, others coordinate teams, others keep administrative processes running. And those roles may evolve over time without the organization losing its ability to function.

At Farox, there's a machine that works remarkably well.

To me, **cooperatives are an answer to the lack of meaning** that often appears when someone asks why a business process exists and the only answer is: "Because that's just how it's done."

People often say cooperatives work because their members are committed. I think that's true. But it's only part of the story.

They also work because they force us to ask a question that many other organizational models rarely encourage: Why am I doing this task?

**That question prevents purpose from being replaced by routine.**

Because when people understand the reason behind what they do, ownership no longer depends on who gave the order. It depends on who decided to take responsibility.
