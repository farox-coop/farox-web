Rome \| October 2026

Starting a text with a place and a date isn't just a formality. It's a way of planting a flag. Of marking a moment in time and saying that something happening in the present should become a turning point between what came before and what comes next. Between what we have and what we want to build.

This year, the PICO Foundation, together with the International Cooperative Alliance and Legacoop, organized a Cooperative AI Bootcamp in Italy. An international gathering that brought together cooperators, researchers, specialists, and innovators to explore how cooperative principles can be applied to the development and governance of artificial intelligence. I had the opportunity to attend and represent Farox and Patio in that conversation.

What we experienced was a much-needed opportunity to step away from our daily routines and think collectively about how the cooperative movement can and should position itself in relation to a technological transformation that is already reshaping the world of work, business models, and the ways value is created and distributed.

Beyond the technical content and proposed solutions, the gathering left us with one central question: **How can we move from being users of technologies developed within systems of economic concentration to actively participating in building alternatives grounded in cooperative values?**

Quite a question, isn't it?

That's why I want to share some of the reflections that emerged throughout the event, so that this article can serve both as an invitation to join the conversation and as a commitment to keep moving in that direction, collectively.

## **Building cooperative AI is as ambitious as it is necessary**

Just as you can't think about planting a seed without considering the conditions of the soil, we as a movement cannot ignore the scale of investment, the concentration of infrastructure and data, and the power of major technology companies. All of these make the challenge of building an alternative grounded in cooperative values enormous.

![Cooperative AI Bootcamp participants working around a table](/images/blog/shaping-AI-through-cooperative-principles-1.webp)

However, if one thing became clear during the Bootcamp, it's that the cooperative movement cannot remain on the sidelines of a technology that is profoundly transforming work and value creation. It needs to become a central actor and help shape the direction of its future development.

This led to one of the most basic, yet fundamental, questions: **What do we mean by cooperative AI?**

Because the discussion cannot stop at the idea of "developing our own models." It must address how cooperative principles can be applied across the entire AI value chain: from infrastructure and data to development, governance, applications, and the distribution of benefits.

And this is where a particularly interesting opportunity opens up. We don't necessarily need to compete with major technology companies in training foundation models. **Understanding the AI value chain allows us to identify where we can make meaningful changes.** There are different points of intervention where cooperatives can contribute solutions, build autonomy, and reduce dependencies.

At this point, I have to say that the initiative to create an international group of practitioners was particularly well conceived. Bringing together people from different countries and disciplines who are already working with AI, combining technical knowledge, organizational experience, and political perspectives, is much more than developing another theoretical framework. It may be the first step toward turning this group into a community of practice capable of driving concrete projects beyond declarations of principles.

**But along the way, we must not forget that this challenge requires the involvement of the entire cooperative movement.** Technology cooperatives have knowledge and capabilities, but they cannot mobilize the resources needed for a transformation of this scale on their own. Building agreements with large cooperative organizations that can provide funding, infrastructure, institutional legitimacy, and opportunities for implementation is essential.

**Without resources and institutional commitments, cooperative AI risks remaining an aspiration.** We need to move from conversation to a collective strategy, with priorities, budgets, working teams, and concrete objectives.

## **Patio is a key player**

Patio is an international network of technology cooperatives with a presence in more than 20 countries, bringing together knowledge, experience, and development capabilities. We're not starting from scratch, and I believe that's the key to what our network can contribute.

Concrete AI development experiences already exist within our network. Some cooperatives are creating products, integrating AI into workflows, researching open models, or building solutions designed to protect organizational data and technological autonomy.

Farox, for example, developed GenIA this year together with Cambá. It's a suite of tools that is beginning to be used in the public sector to strengthen AI governance and technological sovereignty, with a direct impact not just on a company or organization, but on large segments of Argentina's population.

![Presentation on AI governance and technological sovereignty at the Bootcamp](/images/blog/shaping-AI-through-cooperative-principles-2.webp)

But Patio is not a homogeneous group. There is a diversity of perspectives that we need to value. Not every cooperative in the network shares the same view of AI. Some are actively adopting it, others are experimenting cautiously, and others question its impacts or have decided not to use it.

That diversity, far from being an obstacle, is one of our network's greatest strengths. It allows us to develop a critical perspective without assuming that AI adoption is inevitable or always desirable.

This multidisciplinary and open approach allows us to offer technological alternatives grounded in different principles. In the face of concentrated power and dependence on proprietary platforms, Patio cooperatives can contribute open technologies, shared infrastructure, data protection, and solutions that strengthen technological sovereignty.

At this point, I think it's worth pausing because **the concept of technological sovereignty is fundamental**, and we need a shared understanding of what it means. It would be a mistake to approach it as something fragmented, rooted in isolation or individualism, or to assume that technological sovereignty means every cooperative must develop its own AI. Building must always be a collective effort. For us, sovereignty means having the knowledge, capabilities, diversity, and alternatives needed to make autonomous decisions about which technologies to use, how to use them, and under what conditions.

This is precisely why **Patio can serve as a bridge between technical knowledge and the international cooperative movement.** We have an organizational structure and a community that could contribute to the practitioners' group, support pilot projects, and help turn cooperative principles into solutions that can actually be implemented.

*\* These ideas are also reflected in Patio's collective contribution presented to the International Cooperative Alliance in September 2026\.*

## **Innovation is not synonymous with indiscriminate AI adoption**

The many perspectives, voices, and positions within Patio have led to a clear and unavoidable reflection, one we brought to the table at the Rome Bootcamp: **we should not confuse innovation with the indiscriminate adoption of artificial intelligence.**

This is nothing new, but it's always worth repeating that AI is neither neutral nor necessarily beneficial. Its development is shaped by economic interests, political decisions, and business models that we need to examine critically. These forces affect not only the activities of technology cooperatives but are also fundamentally transforming the global structure of work, with direct consequences for our natural ecosystems and social fabric.

That's why the impact on work, data governance, and environmental sustainability must be central to the discussion.

AI can improve productivity and reduce repetitive tasks, but it can also contribute to precarious working conditions, employment uncertainty, and the concentration of economic benefits. People must remain in control of decisions. Automation does not eliminate the need for professional knowledge, human judgment, and accountability for outcomes. If anything, it makes them even more important.

And speaking of decisions, data governance is one of the greatest concerns we've identified in the indiscriminate adoption of new technologies, a situation that cuts across the entire productive economy. From the private to the public sector, many organizations are incorporating AI tools without knowing what information they're sharing, where it's stored, or what technological dependencies they're creating.

![Working group discussing data governance](/images/blog/shaping-AI-through-cooperative-principles-3.webp)

We cannot afford to overlook these issues when every one of these actions involves energy and resource consumption by AI infrastructure, forcing us to continually ask when its use is truly justified. Environmental sustainability must also be part of the debate.

Choosing not to use AI to automate everything that can be automated is also a valid decision. A responsible approach must consider simpler, less expensive alternatives that are better suited to each organization's needs.

The principles of shared ownership, democratic control, autonomy, education, and cooperation can make a distinctive contribution to AI governance.

## **What comes next? Opportunities and next steps**

These kinds of gatherings always leave me moving between two fixed points, like a pendulum: the search for a shared theoretical framework and consensus on key issues, and the strong, almost visceral need to turn ideas into concrete collective action in the short and medium term.

Based on what we worked on during the Rome Bootcamp and the capabilities that already exist within Patio, I came away with several possible lines of action that I believe are important to put into writing. Not just as a record, but because, as I said at the beginning, I want this article to be an open invitation to help collectively build a different paradigm.

1. Actively participate in the practitioners' group, contributing concrete experiences from technology cooperatives and connecting those already developing solutions.  
2. Map existing capabilities within the cooperative movement, identifying AI projects, infrastructure, knowledge, and the needs of different sectors.  
3. Promote an institutional agreement among major cooperative actors, involving the ICA and other organizations, to mobilize resources and sustain a shared agenda.  
4. Identify pilot projects with concrete outcomes, particularly in areas such as data sovereignty, shared infrastructure, open technologies, and applications for cooperatives across different sectors.  
5. Create spaces for training and debate, enabling cooperative leaders, workers, and members to participate in decisions about AI adoption.  
6. Build a research and monitoring agenda to understand how AI is affecting employment, business models, technological autonomy, and the distribution of value.

**This experience reinforced my belief that the cooperative movement has something valuable to contribute to the future of artificial intelligence, but it needs to organize itself to do so.**

The challenge is not necessarily to build a complete alternative to the major technology platforms. It's about identifying where we can intervene effectively, leveraging the capabilities we already have, and building projects that respond to different priorities: autonomy, decent work, economic democracy, and a fairer distribution of value.

From Patio, we have a concrete opportunity to contribute. We have an international network, technical knowledge, practical experience, and a critical perspective on technological development.

**What we need now is to connect those capabilities with the broader cooperative movement, secure resources, and establish long-term commitments.**

Cooperative AI cannot remain merely an aspiration of those of us working in technology. It must become a collective project of the entire cooperative movement.
