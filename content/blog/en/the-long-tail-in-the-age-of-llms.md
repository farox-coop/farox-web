A few years ago, one of the great promises of the Internet was that businesses would no longer have to decide what deserved a place on the shelf.

A physical bookstore has limited space. So does a video rental store. And a television channel has only so many hours in a day. When distribution space is scarce, it makes sense to prioritize what most people are likely to consume: bestsellers, major releases, and hits.

The Long Tail theory, popularized by Chris Anderson, proposed that the Internet changed this logic. If the cost of storing and distributing an additional product approaches zero, there is no longer a need to focus exclusively on what might become a mainstream success.

A digital catalog can contain millions of books, songs, movies, or products. And even if each item in that long tail has very little individual demand, their combined demand can be enormous. **In a sense, the Internet eliminated the shelf.**

Lately, I've been wondering whether LLMs are building a new one.

## From Physical Space to Probability

Suppose someone asks, "What places should I visit in Japan?" There are thousands of possible answers. Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka, and Nara are obvious choices, but there are also hundreds of small cities, towns, rural regions, islands, local festivals, and places that would probably never appear on a conventional list.

An LLM has, at least in theory, the ability to talk about all of them. The problem, as usual, is choosing. AI doesn't return the entire space of possibilities. It decides what to return. And that choice is shaped by probabilities.

When faced with a general question, it's reasonable for the most common, best-documented, and most frequently represented answers in the model's training data to appear first. **Not because the other options don't exist, but because they are less probable.**

This reveals an interesting distinction. The Long Tail helped solve the problem of what could be made available. LLMs bring another question back to the forefront: **what is probable enough to be mentioned?**

The shelf is no longer limited by square footage, but attention remains limited. The new bottleneck is no longer storage or distribution, but the probability of appearing in an answer.

## An Invisible Shelf

One way to think about it is to imagine an infinite warehouse where you can find practically anything you might be looking for. But instead of exploring it ourselves, we talk to someone who enters the warehouse and comes back with five things they think we're likely to find interesting.

The problem is no longer that the other things aren't available. It's that we might never see them.

So while LLMs don't destroy the Long Tail, they change the angle from which we approach it: **the question used to be whether something could be stored and distributed. Now it may be whether something has a high enough probability of emerging in the answer.**

That shift may seem small, but it could significantly change how we discover information. Although this already happened with search engines and recommendation systems, LLMs introduce a different way of experiencing the phenomenon.

## The Paradox: LLMs Can Also Expand the Long Tail

At the same time, there's something that makes this idea considerably more complex: LLMs can also be extraordinary tools for accessing the Long Tail.

Let's return to the example of Japan. Instead of asking, "What places should I visit?", I could ask: "I'm looking for a small town less than two hours from Osaka, with little international tourism, good regional cuisine, and that's worth visiting in winter."

The answer would probably be very different. Suddenly, the model has enough context to move beyond the most obvious options. Something that might previously have required hours of searching, comparing blogs, maps, forums, and reviews can now appear in seconds.

**This means two forces are operating simultaneously.** On the one hand, LLMs dramatically reduce the cost of exploring niches. On the other, when context is limited, their own probabilistic logic can push us toward the most common options.

And this is where things get interesting, because **what this reveals is that LLMs don't "kill" the Long Tail. Instead, they highlight just how important context is when it comes to accessing it.**

When we don't provide enough context, the model has to fill in the gaps. And when it does, it tends to rely on what is most probable.

## From the Long Tail to Cultural Compression

Up to this point, the discussion could simply be about how recommendations work. But I think there's a second layer to the relationship between the Long Tail and LLMs: **what happens when those recommendations don't just reflect existing culture, but begin to reshape it?**

Imagine millions of people asking where to eat in a particular city. If models tend to recommend the same restaurants, those places receive more visitors. More visitors generate more reviews, photos, videos, social media posts, and articles. That increased digital presence can feed search engines, recommendation systems, and eventually future models.

A feedback loop emerges: greater visibility increases the probability of being recommended; more recommendations generate more attention; and more attention creates even greater visibility.

The alternatives don't disappear. They still exist, but they may gradually receive less attention.

**This is where the phenomenon of cultural compression emerges.**

I don't mean that we'll all end up consuming exactly the same things, or that LLMs will automatically eliminate diversity. I'm talking about something more subtle: a system that synthesizes an enormous space of possibilities may end up directing an increasingly large share of our attention toward an increasingly small portion of that space.

The Long Tail is still there. What changes is how much of it we explore.

This phenomenon has a downside because it can compress diversity. But at the same time, with the seemingly endless growth of information we experience every day, we need tools that help us make decisions across such vast ranges of alternatives.

If we sit down at a restaurant with a menu that's over a hundred pages long, we'll probably end up asking the waiter what they recommend.

And that brings us back to the question: what happens to diversity if the waiter always recommends the same dish? How do we break out of that loop?

## Availability Doesn't Guarantee Discovery

The Internet got us used to associating availability with diversity. If everything can be made available, niches have a better chance of surviving. And that's still true. But availability doesn't guarantee discovery.

We can have access to millions of songs and keep listening to the same hundred. We can have millions of books just a click away and end up reading the same titles recommended over and over again. We can have thousands of possible destinations and keep visiting the ones that appear first whenever we ask.

Recommendation systems were already raising this issue before LLMs, but generative models introduce something different.

A list of search results still shows us alternatives. A synthesized answer often gives us a conclusion directly. **And the more convenient that conclusion is, the fewer reasons we have to keep exploring.**

Perhaps the most important change isn't what models know, but how much of the space of possibilities we actually end up seeing.

## The Solution Isn't to Use Less AI

None of this means we should stop using LLMs to discover things. In fact, the opposite is probably true, because they may be among the most powerful tools we've ever had for exploring complex information.

But perhaps we need to think more carefully about how we use them and how we design the systems around them.

Asking more specific questions, introducing constraints, requesting less obvious alternatives, deliberately asking for diversity, comparing answers, or combining models with search and retrieval systems can help expand the space we explore.

It's also important to design tools that optimize not only for relevance or probability, but also for exploration.

Because if our primary interface to an ever-growing amount of information is going to be a machine that predicts what we probably want, then how we define that prediction matters enormously.

## A New Long Tail

The Long Tail emerged, in part, from a reduction in distribution costs. LLMs reduce something else: the cost of navigating information.

This can open up enormous possibilities, but **it can also create a new kind of concentration, one based not on what can fit on the shelf, but on what manages to make it out of the warehouse.**

The Internet made a practically infinite catalog possible. Now we're building intermediaries that decide, token by token, which part of that catalog reaches us.  
The Long Tail will probably continue to exist in this new era. That's why it's so important to ask ourselves: when availability is infinite but attention is mediated by probability, how do we make sure that what lies at the back of the warehouse remains discoverable?

And perhaps there's an even more important question: what happens to our culture when we spend less and less time exploring and increasingly rely on asking what we should look at?

Exploration involves friction. It means taking detours, comparing options, spending time, and coming across something unexpected. Often, that's precisely where discovery happens: finding something that didn't seem relevant at first, stumbling upon a song far removed from what we usually listen to, or having a conversation that takes us somewhere new.

When we increasingly delegate that journey to systems that synthesize, organize, and prioritize information for us, we gain efficiency, but we also remove some of that friction.  
The risk isn't just that we'll always end up seeing what's most popular. It's that we'll grow accustomed to receiving a selection that's already been made for us and, over time, lose some of the habit of exploring the possibilities on our own.

**And that matters because culture is shaped not only by what we choose, but also by what we discover without looking for it.** If the systems mediating our access to information increasingly reduce the possibility of those chance encounters, perhaps the question isn't only how diverse the Long Tail remains, but how much of that diversity we're still capable of discovering.
