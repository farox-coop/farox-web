A few years ago, one of the great promises of the internet was that businesses would no longer have to choose what made it onto a shelf.

A physical bookstore has limited space, and so does a video rental store; a television channel also has a finite number of hours each day. When distribution space is scarce, it makes sense to prioritize what most people are likely to consume: bestsellers, major releases, hits.

The Long Tail theory, popularized by Chris Anderson, proposed that the internet changed this logic because if the cost of storing and distributing one additional product approaches zero, there is no longer a need to choose only what can become a mass-market success.

A digital catalog can contain millions of books, songs, movies, or products, and although each item in that "long tail" may have very little demand individually, the aggregate can be enormous. In a sense, the internet eliminated the shelf.

Lately, I have been wondering whether LLMs are building a new one.

## From physical space to probability

Suppose someone asks, "What places should I visit in Japan?" There are thousands of possible answers. Tokyo, Kyoto, Osaka, and Nara are obvious options, but there are also hundreds of small cities, towns, rural regions, islands, local festivals, and places that would probably never appear on a conventional list.

At least in theory, an LLM can talk about all of them. The problem, as usual, is choosing. AI does not return the entire space of possibilities; it decides what to return. And that choice is shaped by probabilities.

In response to a general question, it is reasonable to expect the answers that are most frequent, best documented, and most represented in the data the model learned from to appear first. Not because the other options do not exist, but because they are less likely to be mentioned.

Here an interesting difference emerges: the Long Tail helped solve the problem of what could be available; LLMs bring another question back to the foreground: what is probable enough to be mentioned?

The shelf is no longer limited by square footage, but attention is still limited. The new bottleneck is no longer storage or distribution, but the likelihood of appearing in an answer.

## An invisible shelf

One way to think about it is to imagine an infinite warehouse where you can find practically anything you want, but instead of walking through it ourselves, we talk to someone who goes inside and comes back with five things they think we are likely to be interested in.

The problem is no longer that those other things are unavailable, but that we may never see them.

So while LLMs do not destroy the Long Tail, they change the angle from which we look at it: before, the question was whether something could be stored and distributed; now, it may be whether something is likely enough to surface in an answer.

That change may seem small, but it can significantly alter how we discover information. This was already happening with search engines and recommendation systems, but LLMs introduce a different way of experiencing it.

## The paradox: LLMs can also expand the Long Tail

At the same time, there is something that makes this idea considerably more complex: LLMs can also be an extraordinary tool for accessing the Long Tail.

Let's return to the example of Japan. Instead of asking, "What places should I visit?", I could ask: "I'm looking for a small town less than two hours from Osaka that would make sense to visit in winter, with limited international tourism and good regional food."

The answer would probably be very different. Suddenly, the model has enough context to move away from the most obvious options. Something that might once have required hours of searching and comparing blogs, maps, forums, and reviews can appear in seconds.

That means two forces are operating at the same time. On one hand, LLMs greatly reduce the cost of exploring niche options. On the other, when context is sparse, their own probabilistic logic can push us toward what is most common.

And this is where things get interesting, because what this brings to light is that LLMs do not "kill" the Long Tail; they foreground how important context is for accessing it.

When we do not provide enough context, the model has to fill it in. And when it does, it tends to do so with what is most likely.

## From the Long Tail to cultural compression

Up to this point, the discussion could simply be about how recommendations work. But I think there is a second layer to this question of the Long Tail and LLMs: what happens when those recommendations do not merely reflect the existing culture, but begin to change it?

Imagine that millions of people ask where to eat in a particular city. If models tend to recommend the same restaurants, those places receive more visitors. More visitors produce more reviews, photos, videos, posts, and articles. That greater digital presence can feed search engines, recommendation systems, and eventually future models.

A feedback loop then forms: a greater digital presence increases the likelihood of being recommended; more recommendations generate more attention; more attention generates an even greater digital presence.

The alternatives do not disappear; they continue to exist, but they could receive progressively less attention.

That is where the phenomenon of cultural compression appears.

I do not mean that we will all consume exactly the same things or that LLMs will automatically eliminate diversity. I mean something subtler: a system that synthesizes a huge space of possibilities may end up concentrating a larger and larger share of our attention on a smaller and smaller part of that space.

The Long Tail is still there; what changes is how much of it we explore.

This phenomenon has a downside because it can compress diversity. Yet in the face of the unending proliferation of information we encounter every day, we need tools that help us choose among such a vast range of alternatives. If we sit down at a restaurant with a menu that is more than a hundred pages long, we will probably end up asking the waiter what they recommend. And there the question comes back: what happens to diversity if the waiter always recommends the same dish? How do we get out of the loop?

## Availability does not guarantee discovery

The internet taught us to associate availability with diversity. If everything can be made available, niche offerings have a better chance of surviving. And that is still true, but availability does not guarantee discovery.

We can have access to millions of songs and always listen to the same hundred songs. We can have millions of books a click away and end up reading the same titles recommended to us again and again. We can have thousands of possible destinations and visit the ones that appear first whenever we ask.

Recommendation systems already posed this problem before LLMs, but generative models add something different. A list of results still shows us alternatives. A synthesized response often gives us a conclusion directly.

And the more convenient that conclusion is, the less reason we have to keep exploring.

Perhaps the most important change is not what models know, but how much of the space of possibilities we end up seeing.

## The solution is not to use less AI

None of this means we should stop using LLMs to discover things. In fact, the opposite is probably true, because they may be among the most powerful tools we have had for exploring complex information.

But perhaps we need to think more carefully about how we use them and how we design the systems around them.

Asking more specific questions, adding constraints, requesting less obvious alternatives, deliberately asking for diversity, comparing answers, or combining models with search and retrieval systems can help expand the space we explore.

It is also important to design tools that optimize not only for relevance or probability, but for exploration as well. If the main interface to an ever-growing amount of information is going to be a machine that predicts what we are likely to want, then how we define that prediction matters enormously.

## A new Long Tail

The Long Tail emerged, in part, from a reduction in the cost of distribution. LLMs reduce something else: the cost of navigating information.

That can open up enormous possibilities, but it can also create a new kind of concentration, based not on what can fit on the shelf, but on what manages to come out of the warehouse.

The internet made a virtually infinite catalog possible. Now we are building intermediaries that decide, token by token, which part of that catalog reaches us.

The Long Tail will probably continue to exist in this new era. That is why it is so important to ask: when availability is infinite but attention is mediated by probability, how do we make sure that what is at the back of the warehouse remains discoverable?

And perhaps there is an even more important question: what happens to our culture as we explore less and less and ask more and more what we should look at?

Exploration involves friction. It means taking a detour, comparing, losing time, and coming across something we did not expect. Often, that is exactly where the unexpected comes from: finding something that did not seem relevant at first glance, discovering a song far removed from what we usually listen to, or having a conversation that takes us somewhere else.

As we increasingly delegate that journey to systems that synthesize, organize, and prioritize for us, we gain efficiency, but we also reduce some of that friction. The risk is not only that we end up seeing what is most popular all the time. It is that we become accustomed to receiving a selection that has already been made for us and, over time, lose some of the habit of exploring the space on our own.

And that matters because culture is built not only from what we choose, but also from what we find without looking for it. If the systems that mediate our access to information increasingly reduce the possibility of coming across something by chance, perhaps the question is not only how diverse the Long Tail remains, but how much of that diversity we are still able to discover.
